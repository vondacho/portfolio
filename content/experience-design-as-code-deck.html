UX in the connected SDLC

Experience Design
as Code

Connecting user evidence and intended experience to discovery, delivery, system design, and production evidence

Visual design · Digital contracts · Git source of truth · AI assistance

UX already shapes the whole software lifecycle. Research reveals needs. Journeys give product work a user-centred structure. Flows, prototypes, content, accessibility, and design-system choices shape implementation.

Experience Design as Code gives those decisions a first-class place in a connected and contract-driven SDLC. Visual tools remain the working environment. A normalised experience model records the delivery obligations and connections that people, engineering tools, and AI need.

Experience Design as Code connects user evidence and intended experience to the same specification backbone as product, domain, architecture, and code.

The handoff problem

Experience meaning disappears between design and delivery

UX creates

Research, journeys, service blueprints, flows, prototypes, content, and accessibility decisions.

Delivery receives

A design link, screenshots, a flat ticket, and assumptions about missing states.

The design handoff usually compresses rich evidence and decisions into a small number of references. A prototype can show the expected path, but may not make loading, empty, error, interrupted, and recovery states explicit.

Research becomes detached from the story it informed. Developers cannot always identify which prototype revision, design-system component, accessibility rule, or content decision applies. Production data rarely reconnects to the original user hypothesis.

A design link and a ticket rarely preserve the evidence, states, and constraints behind the intended experience.

UX contribution

Evidence and experience intent become lifecycle inputs

UX contributes a governed model of who the software serves, what they need, and how the experience should behave.

The visual design remains essential. The digital contract preserves the semantics that delivery must carry forward.

Experience Design as Code does not attempt to encode taste or replace visual exploration. It records what downstream work depends on: the user and outcome, journey position, touchpoints, interaction states, content intent, accessibility requirements, approved components, prototype revision, and evidence.

This makes UX material usable by product, domain, engineering, quality, operations, and AI without transferring design authority away from UX professionals.

The contract captures delivery obligations while visual tools continue to support exploration and design.

Experience digital contract

User and outcomePerson, context, need, and desired change
Journey and flowStep, touchpoint, path, and channel
InteractionLoading, empty, error, success, interruption, and recovery

The contract describes the experience elements that other lifecycle artifacts consume. Stable identifiers connect each element to its source and downstream use.

The model should remain selective. It formalises approved delivery obligations. Exploratory sketches and working notes can remain fluid until the team commits a design to a release.

The experience contract records approved obligations and leaves exploratory design appropriately flexible.

Shared entry point

The user journey connects UX, product, and domain discovery

Research evidenceNeeds, context, friction, and observed behaviour
User journeyGoals, steps, touchpoints, and desired outcomes
Domain behaviourEvents, processes, rules, and responsibility
Delivery modelStories, examples, architecture, and code

The user journey provides the common entry without forcing every discipline into one model. UX owns its integrity and connection to research. Product selects outcomes and release priorities. Event Storming explores the business behaviour involved in achieving those outcomes.

Shared identifiers let each discipline preserve its own concepts while remaining connected to the same user need.

The user journey gives UX, product, and domain work a shared point of reference without merging their responsibilities.

Pivotal bridge

The service blueprint joins frontstage and backstage

UserGoal, journey step, context, and evidence
FrontstageTouchpoints, content, interface actions, and visible feedback
BackstageCommands, domain events, policies, actors, and exceptions
SystemsComponents, integrations, technical contracts, and operations

A journey map focuses on what a person experiences. A service blueprint connects that experience to the people, processes, and systems that make it possible.

This also prevents a common modelling error. A user-visible event such as confirmation displayed and a domain event such as booking revised are connected, but they describe different facts and can occur at different times.

AI can propose a first blueprint from journey and Event Storming models. UX, domain, and engineering participants correct the assumptions and assign responsibility.

The service blueprint connects each visible experience to the domain behaviour and systems responsible for it.

Event Storming connection

UX perspective strengthens all three scopes

Big Picture

User pain, moments of uncertainty, major events, external systems, and organisational hotspots.

Process Modelling

One journey connected to commands, events, policies, exceptions, and backstage dependencies.

System Design

User feedback for decisions, latency, failure, recovery, and availability.

Big Picture Event Storming gives designers a wider view of the domain and organisation. UX research contributes user pain and uncertainty to the landscape.

Process Modelling reveals what happens behind a specific journey. System Design then connects software decisions and failure modes to what the user should see and be able to do. The experience model references the domain events that change the visible state.

UX evidence enriches discovery, while Event Storming exposes the business and system behaviour behind the experience.

Generated tickets inherit stable references to the accepted models. Ticketing continues to coordinate work, but no longer has to carry every meaning in copied prose.

The Story Map preserves the journey, and contextualised tickets bring approved UX material into daily delivery.

DDD and system design

Experience language connects to bounded responsibility

Semantic alignment

User-facing terminology connects to the correct bounded context without exposing internal technical language.

System responsibility

C4, LikeC4, PlantUML, and API contracts identify the components and interactions supporting each touchpoint.

The same word can mean different things in different bounded contexts. UX content should use language intended for the user while retaining a clear relationship to the responsible domain model.

Architecture shows which systems and components support a journey step. Technical contracts define the required data and operations. Performance, availability, privacy, and security constraints can then link to the experience they affect.

DDD protects meaning, while system design assigns the components and contracts responsible for the experience.

Practical representation

A compact contract connects one journey step

id: ux.booking-change.select-alternative
journey: booking-change
user_outcome: Find an acceptable alternative
evidence: research.booking-change.07
prototype: { node: "381:2204", revision: approved-2026-09 }
states: [loading, available, unavailable, price-changed, error, success]
accessibility:
  keyboard: required
  screen_reader_feedback: required
references:
  eventstorm: booking-change-requested
  storymap: change-booking
  examplemap: alternative-selection
  component: booking-option-selector

This example records what downstream delivery needs for one journey step. The visual design tool remains the source for the visual node. Git records the accepted revision, states, accessibility obligations, evidence, and links to the other lifecycle models.

A new visual revision becomes authoritative for delivery when the corresponding contract change passes review. This creates a clear handoff without turning visual design into YAML.

The contract links a visual revision to evidence, states, accessibility, and the related lifecycle models.

GitOps extension

The experience contract gains a reconciliation loop

DeclareApproved journeys, states, components, content, and evidence links
ReviewUX and connected owners approve meaning and impact
AutomateValidate, publish, update tickets, and select tests
ReconcileDetect design, code, test, and experience drift

GitOps-style automation keeps consumers aligned with the accepted experience contract. It can validate references, publish blueprints, update governed ticket fields, check component versions, and select affected tests.

Safe projections can regenerate automatically. A difference that changes intended experience becomes a review proposal. Runtime behaviour can challenge the hypothesis, but it should not silently rewrite the design contract.

Automation keeps UX intent connected to delivery and raises meaningful drift for human review.

Adoption path

One journey can prove the integration model

01Select

Choose a journey where design intent regularly disappears during delivery.

02Connect

Link one blueprint, pivotal event, slice, prototype, states, and component.

03Automate

Generate ticket context, publish the blueprint, or validate component revisions.

04Learn

Measure late design questions, missing states, accessibility gaps, and translation effort.

Begin with one meaningful journey and an immediate workflow benefit. Connect the service blueprint to one release slice and its pivotal event, examples, prototype revision, and responsible component.

Avoid formalising every research note or sketch. Add stronger validation when the design becomes a committed delivery contract. Expand after designers and delivery teams trust the flow.

Start with one journey, remove one painful handoff, and strengthen the contract as decisions mature.
